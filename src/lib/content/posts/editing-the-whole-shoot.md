A shoot is not one photo. It is a few dozen frames of the same dog on the same
dock in the same light, and they need to look like they belong together.

They are not the same photo, though. The light shifts between frames, the dog
moves, one shot has a mooring post standing in the water and the next does not.
Copy the layers from the first onto the second and you land close, then spend
longer fixing the places it does not fit than you saved by copying.

What a retoucher does instead is repeat the process rather than the settings, and
get quicker with every pass. The first frame takes the longest because it is
where the decisions happen. By the tenth you are not really deciding any more.
You are fitting a treatment you already trust to a picture that is slightly
different from the last one.

Editmamei works in that shape. You spend the time getting the first photo right,
then carry the process forward and let the exact values follow each frame.

That is what a template is, and it covers the creative half of a shoot. There is
a second feature for the half that is only filing.

## Templates carry the look

What gets saved is not a preset or a stack of sliders. It is the recipe: what the
look is for, the conditions it assumes, the ordered steps with their real values,
which settings should flex from photo to photo and which must not, and how to
tell whether it landed.

Applied to the next frame, Claude works through that recipe rather than starting
over. It reads the photo first, then fits the treatment to what is actually
there. A shaded frame gets more exposure. A post in a different place gets a
different selection. A frame with no dog in it skips the steps that were about
the dog. The values move; the intent does not.

That is also what keeps the intent alive at frame thirty. A recipe that says keep
the dog tan rather than orange holds long after you have stopped watching
closely.

## Batch carries the filing

Some of the work is not creative at all. Cropping a finished set to 4:5 for
Instagram, resizing, exporting. It is identical on every file and it still has to
happen.

Photoshop has its own batch engine for this, and Editmamei can compile the job
and hand the whole folder over at once rather than opening, cropping, exporting
and closing each file in turn.

## What that saves

Editmamei works through your AI subscription, so every edit draws on your plan.
Both features exist to keep that draw down, and I wanted to know by how much.

Editing one photo from a plain description, with nothing prepared in advance,
took a little over twenty minutes. Claude looks at the picture, settles on a
treatment, tries it, checks the result, changes its mind, tries again. Most of
the cost sits in that loop.

Then the finished look, saved as a template and run across ten photos.

The ten finished in about the same time as the one, for about the same budget.
The first photo pays for the decision and the rest inherit it, and the effect
compounds: the step from one photo to three still costs real money per frame,
while the frames after that cost very little.

The batch side has a different shape. On ten files, handing the set over came in
around a third of the time of working through them individually. File by file,
every photo you add is another round of instructions. Handing the set over stays
flat. Ten files was three instructions, and a hundred files would still be about
three.

## A template knows what it is for

A recipe carries the conditions it was built for, and that turns out to be worth
as much as the settings.

I built one on a clear bright morning, then pointed it at a different day:
heavily overcast, white sky, no blue anywhere. Before touching a file, Claude
read the conditions written into the template, held them up against the new
photos and said this was the wrong template for this shoot.

That is the part I would not want to give up. We ran it anyway, to see what wrong
looked like, and it was exactly as advertised. The treatment applied perfectly,
the same on every frame, no drift at all. It was also wrong on every frame with
much sky in it, all of which came out looking like the air was full of smoke.

The reason is specific. On a blue sky, the warmth in that recipe is held in check
by the work it does on the blue channel. An overcast sky has no blue in it, so
nothing balances the warmth and it lands on a flat grey field. Different light
needs its own template rather than a nudge to one slider.

A template that knows what it is for can tell you when you are about to use it
somewhere it does not belong. Before fifty photos come out wrong, rather than
after.
